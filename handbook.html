Bootcamp

Before you go anywhere, you get ready. Bootcamp is 16 activities across four phases, and it is 25% of your course grade — each activity is scored on its own. You will have a real grade in this class before you ever set foot at a placement.

Why this exists

Every one of these activities produces something you will actually use: a resume you'll apply with, a pitch you'll deliver at the capstone, an interview you'll survive, a plan you'll be held to. Nothing in bootcamp is busywork designed to fill three weeks. If you can't see how an activity connects to the job, ask — there's an answer.

Special Course Policies

A fully signed Training Agreement (student, parent/guardian, employer, and school coordinator) must be on file before your first day of work — no exceptions. Performance or conduct violations are addressed per the Training Agreement and may result in removal from the program.

Documentation Gate — you must be current to work

Your weekly timesheet and reflection are due Sunday at 11:59 PM. If they are not submitted, you do not go to work that week until they are complete.

Placement track: not current, not on the schedule. You report to class and finish your timesheet and reflection instead of going to your site.

Enterprise track: not current, not remote. Working off campus is a privilege earned by being current. If you're behind, you report to class on your A day until you're caught up. Bulldog Media students also lose equipment and studio access until current.

This is not a rule invented for school. Every contractor, freelancer, and hourly employee gets paid by submitting their documentation on time.

If a transportation barrier or an IEP/504 accommodation affects your ability to submit, talk to your teacher. Those are handled as accommodations before the gate is ever applied — a ride that falls through is an attendance problem to solve, not a rule violation.

The scale — used for every single graded item

Every graded item is worth 4 points. Your percentage is just the points you earned out of 4 — nothing is curved, rounded up, or translated.

4Excellent — exceeded the standard; took initiative beyond what was asked100%
3.5Approaching Excellent — clearly past "meets," not consistently excellent yet87.5%
3Meets — performed to the standard a paying employer expects. This is the target.75%
2Does Not Meet — inconsistent; a supervisor would have to correct you50%
1Well Below Standard — would jeopardize continued employment25%
What "meets" actually means — read this carefully

"Meets" isn't a throwaway. It means you performed to the standard a paying employer expects, which is a real bar. But because every item is out of 4, a 3 comes out to 75% — so meeting the standard is not the same as an A. A 4 is for going beyond what was asked, and that's what moves your grade up.

The practical version: doing exactly what's required earns you a passing, unremarkable grade. Doing more than what's required is how you earn a strong one. That's also how raises and references work, which is the point.

"No Evidence" is not a 1. If nothing was submitted, it's entered as Incomplete — a 1 means the work was seen and fell well short, while Incomplete means nothing arrived yet. Incompletes can be turned in late for partial credit and only become zeros at the end of the quarter.

1. Bootcamp — 25%

Each of the 16 bootcamp activities is scored separately. Finished in the first few weeks of the course.

2. Weekly Tasks — 25%

Every week you submit a timesheet and a reflection, and each one gets its own score. "Week 1 Timesheet" and "Week 1 Reflection" are two different grades. Over a semester this is where most of your grades come from — which means consistency matters more than any single item.

The test your reflection has to pass

Read it back and ask: could this have been written by someone who never went to my placement? If yes, it's a 2. Your reflections are also the raw material for your capstone pitch — the students who write real ones in September have something to say in December.

These are the highest-stakes items in the course

With only two evaluations, each one is worth about 12% of your final grade — roughly the same as fifteen weekly reflections combined. The person filling it out sees you a few hours a week, and what they see is entirely up to you. Everything about how you show up — on time, phone away, asking for more work — ends up on that form.

Enterprise track: your evaluation comes from the Quarterly Review Board, where you present your work to a panel of adults, plus at least one client or advisor feedback form. Board dates: Oct 15 · Dec 16 · Mar 11, with the fourth folded into the May capstone.

If you fall behind — the escalation ladder

Applied the same way to everyone. The count resets each quarter.

MissWhat happens
1stYou complete the missing work during that day's block. No call home. Everyone has one bad week.
2ndThe gate is enforced — no placement or no remote work that day — and an email goes home the same day.
3rdPhone call home, a written improvement plan signed by you and your guardian, and a two-week check-in. Your supervisor is informed, because at a real job this is where a manager would know.

The honest summary

Half of this grade — Bootcamp and Weekly Tasks — is almost entirely within your control and comes down to doing the work on time. The other half depends on how you actually perform in front of adults who don't have to be impressed by you. Students who fail this course rarely fail because the work was too hard. They fail because they stopped submitting.

Section 4
The Capstone

One afternoon at the end of the semester where your work becomes visible to the people who might hire, recommend, or mentor you next. 25% of your grade, in three parts. Everything in bootcamp builds toward this.

Part 1 — Elevator Pitch (1:30)

Where: the Black Box — the small stage in the Fine Arts Center. A real stage with a seated audience listening to you specifically.

A one minute thirty second pitch on yourself and your Field Experience. Live, timed, no notes on a phone — and fully prepared. There is no prompt to react to and no questions to field. Just your 90 seconds, done well.

Two things have to come through: why this experience mattered, and how it is shaping where you go next. Any honest answer works on that second one — including "I found out this field isn't for me," which takes more guts to say than a comfortable answer and is one of the most useful results this course produces.

A structure that works for the prepared 1:30

~15 secWho and where — your name, placement, and cluster. Fast, no wind-up.
~30 secWhat you actually did — not your title. A specific project or responsibility you owned. Specific beats impressive.
~30 secWhat you learned or how you grew — reference something real: a mistake, a skill that clicked, a moment you were wrong. This is the part people remember.
~15 secWhat's next — where this points you. Close with confidence, not "yeah, that's it."
Figuring out what to say — use the Prep Sheet

The Pitch Prep Sheet walks you through prompts for each piece of the pitch, with room to draft and a worked example. Your teachers will hand it out. Broad strokes of what it asks you to think about:

  • A challenge or setback you faced — and what you did about it
  • A person at your placement who taught you something
  • A time you were wrong, corrected, or surprised
  • Advice for next year's student going into your placement
  • How this changed your plans — or confirmed them
  • A skill you didn't expect to need

You won't use all of it. Ninety seconds is about 200–230 words, so you'll use three or four of these answers at most. Work through all of them anyway — that's how you find your best material instead of settling for the first thing you thought of. Your weekly reflections are the raw material. That's what they've been for all along.

Part 2 — Trade Show (BFAC Lobby)

You staff a station in the BFAC Lobby. Guests circulate, and when someone stops you give them a 60–90 second walkthrough of your work, then take their follow-up questions. You'll do it over and over, for people who know nothing about your placement.

The skill being tested isn't public speaking — it's doing it well the tenth time, to someone who just walked up. What's scored: your pop-up presentation, how you handle questions, your station setup (scored before doors open), your professionalism, and whether you can adjust how you talk to a supervisor versus a sophomore versus an administrator.

  • Every station needs a name placard (name, placement, cluster) readable from a few feet away, a resume, and your portfolio artifacts, labeled
  • Set up completely before doors open, not while guests are walking in
  • Professional dress — the same bar as your mock interview
  • Staff your station the whole event. Phone away. Stand up when someone approaches
Section 4 · Capstone

Part 3 — Portfolio

Your portfolio is what's on your station and what backs up your pitch. There is no required list of contents — and that's deliberate. A welding student and an accounting student shouldn't be handing in the same portfolio.

Instead of a checklist you get a standard: bring evidence of what you actually did, enough to show range, organized so a stranger understands it, and be able to explain every piece. How you meet that standard is your call.

The five questions your portfolio has to answer

  1. What did you actually do here? Not what the company does — what you made, fixed, wrote, built, or contributed to. The most common failure is a beautiful display about the organization with no evidence of you in it.
  2. Can you show more than one kind of thing? One artifact repeated four times isn't range.
  3. Can you prove you grew? A before-and-after is the most convincing artifact in any portfolio. Your first attempt next to your tenth.
  4. Would a stranger understand it without you? Label everything — one line each. While you talk to one guest, two more are reading your table.
  5. Are you allowed to show it? Ask your supervisor early, not the week of.
Start collecting in week one, not week fourteen

This is the most important sentence in this section. You cannot build this at the end. The photo you didn't take, the draft you didn't save, the project thrown out in October — those are gone. Spend five minutes every Friday saving something from that week, even if you're not sure you'll use it. Students who do this have too much to choose from. Students who don't spend the last week apologizing for an empty table.

Confidentiality — the one rule with no flexibility

Patient names or health information. Client financials. Student records. Anything under a non-disclosure agreement. Anyone's personal information visible in the background of a photo. None of it goes in your portfolio — not blurred, not "just one page," not "nobody will recognize them." Including it is an automatic 1 on professionalism and the item is removed.

When unsure, the answer is always the same: ask your supervisor first. Nobody has ever gotten in trouble for asking. And you can almost always document the work without the data — a blank version of the form, a screenshot with fields emptied, a photo of your hands and the equipment instead of the screen.

What doesn't count

The syllabus, your timesheet, blank course handouts, a photo of the company sign, the company's own brochures, or a generic "what I learned" paragraph with no artifact attached. Your resume, pitch video, and skills assessment do belong at your station — just don't let them be the portfolio.

Not sure what counts for your placement?

The Portfolio Guide has concrete artifact ideas for all seven career clusters — what works in health sciences versus IT versus the trades — plus the full scoring rubric. Ask for a copy and read it in your first two weeks at your placement.

If your placement makes documentation genuinely hard — some workplaces say no to photos, copies, and taking anything home — that's a real situation and it's solvable, but only if you raise it in October instead of December.

Student Responsibilities

The student agrees to:

  1. Maintain regular attendance at school and the Field Experience. Students must attend school on days they report to their Field Experience. Documented absences (e.g., appointments) may be approved by the Field Experience Coordinator.
  2. Notify both the Field Experience Coordinator and the employer in advance of any absence or schedule change.
  3. Provide their own transportation unless otherwise arranged.
  4. Comply with all District policies (bps101.net/policies), including conduct, attendance, and academic expectations.
  5. Comply with all employer policies, including workplace confidentiality, honesty, conduct, dress, safety, and attendance.
  6. Demonstrate appropriate employability skills, including punctuality, reliability, professional communication, and respect for workplace expectations.
  7. Maintain satisfactory academic standing. Failure to do so may result in loss of privileges, suspension, or removal from the program.
  8. Complete all required coursework, documentation, and reflections associated with the Field Experience course.
  9. Report concerns, safety issues, or conflicts promptly to the Field Experience Coordinator.
  10. Remain enrolled at BHS and in the Field Experience course; withdrawal from either will result in termination of the placement.
  11. Remain with the assigned employer for the duration of the placement. Failure to complete the placement due to performance may result in removal from the program and a failing grade.
  12. Complete a minimum of 60 hours of workplace experience and continue participation through the end of the academic period.

The District may remove a student from Field Experience at its discretion based on academic, behavioral, or safety concerns.

Risk Acknowledgment and Reporting

  • Field Experience placements typically occur in third-party workplaces that are not owned, operated, or controlled by the District.
  • While the District reviews placements for general safety and legal compliance, it cannot monitor all conditions or foresee all risks that may arise in a workplace environment.
  • Workplace risks may include, but are not limited to, injury, inappropriate conduct, harassment (including sexual harassment), discrimination, or other behavior inconsistent with District expectations.
  • The employer is responsible for maintaining a safe, lawful workplace and for enforcing its own policies, including those related to harassment and nondiscrimination.
  • The student must report any safety concern, inappropriate conduct, or policy violation immediately to both the employer and the Field Experience Coordinator.
  • The parent/guardian must notify the Field Experience Coordinator promptly of any concerns affecting the student’s safety or participation.
  • The District will respond to reported concerns, which may include investigation, coordination with the employer, reassignment, or removal from the placement.
  • Participation is voluntary; a student may pause or discontinue participation due to safety concerns without penalty while the District reviews the situation.

Non-Discrimination

All parties agree to comply with applicable federal and state non-discrimination laws.

Driving Acknowledgment (if applicable)

Students are responsible for securing a BHS parking spot via the official process. If released school time does not match internship hours, the student may return home but remains responsible for meeting all hour requirements.

  • You may stop and call. If something is unsafe or inappropriate, you can pause participation without penalty while it is reviewed. Report it to your employer and the Coordinator immediately — not next week.
